Gernreich writing

A script in which every letter is two steps on a square grid, and every word is one unbroken line. Each letter starts where the one before it ended, so a sentence draws itself as a single path that wanders across the page.

Whose work this is. The Gernreich writing, its alphabet and the drawings under The originals are Michael Carnright's. The writer and reader on this page, the redrawn chart and the second drawing of Article 1 are by Claude Code, built from his alphabet chart.

How it works

A pen on a square grid can move one step in eight directions: across, up and down, or diagonally. A letter is two such steps. Two steps that double back on themselves make no shape, which leaves exactly 28 shapes, and the alphabet uses every one of them: the 26 letters, KH, and a 28th drawn in the chart with a marked R (shown here as Ř; its sound is not recorded).

A shape can be traced either way, so the writer chooses the direction of each letter to keep the line going and keep it from running into itself. The same word can come out as several different paths; ONE is drawn five ways in the originals. A reader needs only the starting point, marked with a dot: from there every two steps is one letter.

The alphabet

The chart groups the letters by how far apart a letter's two ends are, as the square of that distance: 1, 2, 4, 5 or 8 units². The groups follow how often the letters are used in English. The ten most common, E T A O I N S H R D, are the two longest groups, and the rarest, J K Q V X Z, are the shortest.

Write

Type anything. Letters outside the alphabet are left out; KH is written as one letter.

Draw as

Read

Draw a path: tap a point to start, then tap one of the eight points around the end of the line to go on. Every two steps is read as a letter.
